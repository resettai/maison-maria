Melanoma in Norway: the numbers

A country with long winters has one of the highest rates of skin cancer in the world. The reasons are behavioural, which means they can change.

October 2026 · 5 min read

In 2025, 3,097 people in Norway were diagnosed with melanoma, according to the Cancer Registry of Norway. The incidence is around twenty times what it was in the 1950s, and only Australia and New Zealand sit higher on the list. Among women, melanoma is the most common cancer before the age of 50.

How a northern country ends up here

The Norwegian pattern is intermittent, intense exposure: pale skin that spends nine months covered, then a fortnight in the Mediterranean, long summer evenings on a boat, and spring skiing at altitude where snow reflects up to 80 percent of the UV back at the face. Sunburn in childhood and bursts of exposure on untanned skin are the strongest risk factors, and the Norwegian calendar is built from them. Solarium use, now restricted but common for decades, added to it.

What the UV index says

On a clear June day in Oslo the midday UV index reaches 6 to 7, the same as a spring day in Rome. At that level unprotected fair skin burns in around 20 minutes. From April to September, the index is 3 or higher for most of the day, which is the threshold at which the World Health Organization recommends protection. In northern Norway the peak is lower, around 4 to 5, but the days are longer. The sun guide has the full table.

The good news in the numbers

Found early, melanoma is one of the most survivable cancers: five-year relative survival above 90 percent when it is diagnosed before it has spread. Found late, it is one of the least. The rule for checking: A for asymmetry, B for irregular border, C for more than one colour, D for diameter over 6 millimetres, E for evolving. A mole that changes is a mole to show a doctor.
